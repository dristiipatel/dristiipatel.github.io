Twenty-four neutrinos that started a new kind of astronomy

5 October 2026 · 2 min read · supernova, neutrinos, intro

When a massive star collapses, about 99% of the released energy leaves as neutrinos. In February 1987 three detectors recorded about two dozen of them from a supernova in a neighbouring galaxy, a few hours before the light arrived. Detectors built since then hope to see the next one with many more events.

Neutrinos from a collapsing star

A massive star runs out of fuel and its iron core collapses. The core compresses to nuclear density, and the gravitational binding energy released is around $3\times10^{46}$ joules, far more than the Sun will emit in its entire life. About 99% of it leaves as neutrinos over roughly ten seconds. The visible explosion carries a tiny remainder.

Neutrinos escape the collapsing core much more easily than light does, so they carry the earliest and most direct information about what is happening in there.

23 February 1987

Supernova 1987A exploded in the Large Magellanic Cloud, about 168,000 light-years away. Three underground detectors recorded neutrino events: Kamiokande II (11 events), IMB (8) and Baksan (5), about two dozen in total, all within roughly 13 seconds. The neutrinos arrived a few hours before the first optical sighting, because light has to fight its way out of the star while neutrinos leave almost freely.

Twenty-four events is not many. They were enough to confirm the basic picture of core collapse, to roughly check the total energy and temperature of the burst, and to put limits on neutrino properties. Part of the 2002 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for the detection of cosmic neutrinos, including those from this supernova.

What a Galactic supernova would offer

A supernova in our own Galaxy would send thousands of events to a large detector instead of a handful. The time profile of the burst, the neutrino energy spectrum and the flavour content would all be measurable. These depend on the physics of the collapsing core and on how the neutrinos change flavour on the way out, which is where the earlier post on matter effects in the Sun comes back: dense matter in a supernova affects flavour conversion too, and different detector materials see different channels.

The problem is timing. Galactic supernovae happen a few times per century, and nobody knows when. The community has built the SNEWS network to send an early alert to telescopes within minutes of a neutrino burst, since neutrinos arrive before the light.

Going deeper

A software package called SNEWPY turns supernova simulation outputs into expected event rates for specific detectors. A module for the diffuse supernova neutrino background, the sum over all past supernovae, is currently under review for that package, and the next post describes what that background is.

A question to take away

If a supernova happened in the Galaxy tomorrow, which detector materials would be most informative, and why would having several different ones matter more than having one huge one?
