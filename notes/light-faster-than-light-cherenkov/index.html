Light faster than light? Cherenkov radiation and the muon on the table

30 October 2026 · 2 min readdetectorsCherenkovintro

Nothing travels faster than light in a vacuum, but light itself slows down in water, and fast particles do not. A charged particle moving faster than light does in the medium emits a cone of blue light, the optical version of a sonic boom. Large neutrino detectors are built to catch that cone.

The sonic boom analogy

A boat moving faster than the waves it makes drags a V-shaped wake behind it. A supersonic aircraft makes a shock cone in the same way. Light in water travels at about $c/1.33$, a quarter slower than in a vacuum. A charged particle with enough energy moves faster than that, and it leaves an electromagnetic wake: Cherenkov light.

The angle gives information

The light comes out on a cone around the direction of motion, with the angle set by $\cos\theta=1/(n\beta)$. For a particle close to the speed of light in water, that is about 41 degrees. So a ring of light seen on the wall of a detector gives the direction of the particle, and the ring's brightness tells how much energy it had.

How this is used for neutrinos

Neutrinos are invisible, but now and then one hits a nucleus or an electron in the detector and sets a charged particle in motion. That particle makes Cherenkov light, and photomultiplier tubes record it. Super-Kamiokande, SNO and many other detectors are tanks of water (ordinary or heavy) lined with thousands of those tubes. The detector never sees the neutrino, only the light from what the neutrino kicked.

Scintillator detectors work differently. They produce much more light per event, which gives better energy resolution, but the light comes out in all directions and the pointing information is mostly lost. Choosing between the two is a long-standing trade-off in the field.

Muons on the table

Cosmic rays hitting the atmosphere produce a steady rain of muons: roughly one per square centimetre per minute at sea level. A muon is a good test beam for a Cherenkov detector, since it is charged, fast and free. A small tank or a block of plastic with a photomultiplier can already show them. A live demonstration where each flash corresponds to a particle passing through is a nice way to show a non-specialist how particle detectors work.
