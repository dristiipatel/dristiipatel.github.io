The faintest glow in the Universe: neutrinos from every supernova that ever happened

9 October 2026 · 2 min readsupernovaDSNBintro

Massive stars have been exploding throughout cosmic history, and each explosion sent out a burst of neutrinos. Added up, they form a faint, steady background. It has been predicted for decades and is close to the limit of what current detectors can find.

Counting the explosions

Somewhere in the observable Universe, a massive star collapses roughly once per second. Each collapse releases a burst of neutrinos for about ten seconds. Most of these bursts are billions of light-years away and no single one could ever be seen. But they do not disappear: their neutrinos keep travelling, redshifted by the expansion of the Universe, and they fill space as a diffuse background.

This is the diffuse supernova neutrino background (DSNB). In terms of numbers, a few events per year in the largest detectors is the kind of rate being discussed.

Why it is useful

It works a bit like a census. The size of the background depends on how often stars collapsed over cosmic history, so it tells how star formation behaved over time. It also depends on how many massive stars collapse into black holes without a bright explosion, which is hard to learn from telescopes because such collapses are dark. A neutrino signal could help find them.

Why it is hard

The signal is a handful of events per year, sitting among backgrounds from atmospheric neutrinos and from radioactivity. The standard channel is inverse beta decay, where an electron antineutrino hits a proton and produces a positron and a neutron. The neutron helps here: if a detector can recognize the neutron (for example by capturing it on gadolinium, which emits a gamma cascade), many backgrounds can be rejected. That is the main idea behind adding gadolinium to Super-Kamiokande.

A good beginner project

The calculation of the expected signal is a nice exercise in how astrophysics and particle physics combine. The ingredients are a supernova neutrino spectrum, the cosmic rate of core collapses as a function of redshift, an integral over the redshift range with the right energy shift, and finally the detector cross section and background. Reproducing a published sensitivity table from scratch takes a few weeks, and it helps more than only reading about it. A later post covers what such a reproduction looks like in practice.

A question to take away

What would it mean if the measured background were noticeably lower than the one predicted from the cosmic star formation rate?
