Looking for a sterile neutrino in solar neutrinos

Manuscript in preparation · with Dr. Biswaranjan Behera, IISc · not yet peer reviewed

Solar boron-8 neutrinos between about 2 and 5 MeV have never been measured in real time. A deuterated liquid scintillator detector could reach that range. I calculated what a sterile neutrino would do to these neutrinos, and how long such a detector would need to see it. This page is a short, plain version of the paper.

The question

Neutrinos made in the Sun's core come out as one type, the electron neutrino. On the way out, the Sun's own matter changes some of them into other types, and the chance that a neutrino is still an electron neutrino when it leaves is called the survival probability, Pee. At low energy it is about 0.55 and at high energy about 0.30. Between roughly 2 and 5 MeV it is changing from one to the other, and that is the stretch nobody has measured well. Existing detectors lose it to backgrounds or to their energy thresholds.

If a light sterile neutrino exists, it could bend the curve in exactly this stretch. A deuterium-based detector can see it, because the reaction of an electron neutrino with deuterium has a low threshold (1.44 MeV). So the question is simple to say: what would the dip look like, and could a detector like that tell it from the standard curve?

What I did

I wrote a solver that evolves three neutrino states (electron, a second active type, and the sterile one) outward through realistic solar density profiles, and tracks which state the neutrino is in at each step. I took one benchmark case, with a mass splitting of 1.2 × 10−5 eV² and a small mixing angle (sin²2θ14 = 0.005). I also did the same for non-standard interactions, which can also distort the curve.

What came out

  • The sterile case gives a dip of about 46% at the bottom, near 2.5 MeV. Non-standard interactions give a smooth, nearly flat shift instead (about −0.02 to −0.035 across the window). The shapes are different, so in principle the two can be told apart.
  • For a 1 kton heavy-water detector running for 10 years, the sterile case would stand apart from the standard one at about 7.9σ, counting statistical error only. After adding a SNO-like fiducial cut and a photodisintegration background, it is about 6.3σ.
  • I compared four deuterated liquid scintillator media (D2O, and D2O with LAB, Gd-LAB or Te-LAB). The precision on Pee changes by only about 1% between them. The reasons to pick one over another lie elsewhere, such as neutron tagging.
  • I compared this with the elastic-scattering channel at JUNO. In the cases I looked at, the two are competitive and neither is clearly ahead. One difference is that the charged-current channel measures Pee directly, without the mixing with other flavours that elastic scattering has.
  • A standard analytic formula for sterile neutrinos (Giunti and Li) gives a correction of only about 0.14% here. The full calculation gives 46%. That is not a contradiction. The formula assumes the sterile mass splitting is much larger than the solar one, and here it is about six times smaller.

How I checked it

  • Setting the sterile mixing to zero gives back the standard formula to about 6 × 10−5 in Pee.
  • At the Sun's surface, the eigenvectors match the vacuum mixing matrix.
  • The dip does not change when I use 300, 500 or 800 radial steps.
  • Two checks that do not assume the neutrino follows one eigenstate agree with the main solver: a transfer-matrix calculation (within 1.4 × 10−3 in Pee) and a direct integration of the Schrödinger equation (within 3 × 10−4).

Two bugs turned up along the way. My first version ordered the states by mass instead of by flavour, which made the tracking mislabel states and moved the dip by about 0.2 MeV. I had also left out a cos²θ13 factor in the matter term, which moves the dip by about 0.13 MeV. I wrote about both in a note on the Hamiltonian.

What I have not done

The benchmark is not a fit. I have not checked it against global analyses of solar and reactor data, and it may already be in tension with existing SNO and Super-Kamiokande spectra. I present the result as the sensitivity of a deuterium detector to a distortion of this shape, and not as a prediction that it exists.

The JUNO comparison is indicative. I simplified its backgrounds and efficiencies, and my own detector has no signal-efficiency factor. The oscillation parameters match NuFIT 4.0 and Borexino's choices, and I haven't propagated the small difference to NuFIT 5.0. The significances are statistical, with simple background models.
